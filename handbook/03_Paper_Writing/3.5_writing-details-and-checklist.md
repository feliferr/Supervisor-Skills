# 3.5 Writing Details and Checklist

The most important thing about a paper is the choice of topic, followed by novelty and contribution, and then experimental results. Only when all three are in place should you move on to writing the paper.

## Why Is Topic Selection the Most Important? A Good Problem Is the Starting Point of Good Research

- The best problem opens up a whole field and drives the development of an industry, like the Nobel Prize or the Turing Award.
- The four Turing Award winners in databases: the father of databases, the father of relational databases, the father of transaction theory, and the father of database systems.
- The second-best problem sparks a wave of enthusiasm and gets widely used, such as Spark and vLLM.
- The third-best problem becomes a research hotspot with many citations. In the DB field, more than 100 citations is already good research (don't compare with AI; the fields differ, and it would be comparing apples to oranges), more than 1,000 is extremely rare, and more than 10,000 happens once in a decade.
- I will set aside time later to talk specifically about how to choose a topic.

## DB Sets a High Bar for Novelty and Contribution

You must give reviewers a sense of "aha". More important still is that readers learn something and that the paper has substance. How do you judge whether novelty and contribution are high?

- If the sub-question or method for this problem could be thought up within ten minutes, the novelty is insufficient.
- If people in the small circle of peers would or would not have thought of it at first glance, then the novelty is sufficient.

## Experimental Results Are an Important Measure of Whether the Proposed Method or System Is Effective

- If the experimental results are poor, the paper may still have value, since it shows the problem is hard, but it is not good research work.
- DB sets a high bar for experimental results: a performance improvement of at least 50%, and preferably 10% or more in accuracy/precision.

## DB Sets an Extremely High Bar for Writing

Only after the three conditions of a novel topic, strong novelty, and good experimental results are met can excellent writing get the paper accepted. So how do you write a paper well? A detailed checklist follows below; here is a highly condensed version, similar to a paper's Abstract.

- **Logic is the most critical**: The paper's logic must be clear so that readers follow the line of thought as they read and do not develop other doubts.
- **Grasp the main thread**: The paper should introduce the whole first and then the details, so readers grasp the big picture before looking at the details. Whether it is the Framework (the overall introduction to the whole paper) or the first subsection of each chapter, always present the overall view first and then unfold the details.
- **Paragraphs are connected to each other**
- **Each paragraph has a leading text, and the whole paragraph revolves around one topic**
- **Sentences are related to each other**
- **Figures and text complement each other**: A good system architecture figure plays a vital role. If you cannot draw the figure clearly, you have not thought it through, and you cannot possibly write it clearly. It is like taking a course: if you cannot draw its core content, you have not understood it at all.
- **A running example throughout the paper**: Sometimes the idea is hard to grasp, but an example that conveys it intuitively makes it much easier for readers to understand.
- **Self-contained**: The paper should be written clearly enough that readers can understand it without relying on knowledge from other papers.
- **The paper should be focused**, not scattered; make sure not to include irrelevant content.

## English Grammar Issues

You can install grammar-checking plugins on Overleaf, such as Grammarly and Overleaf's built-in grammar checker, which can catch spelling mistakes, simple third-person-singular errors, and so on.

### Use Words Accurately

- Capitalization
- the / a / an
- Singular and plural

### Third-Person Singular and Articles

A sentence cannot have two verbs, and it cannot have two clauses.

Word choice should be idiomatic and reasonable; do not just look something up in a dictionary and drop it in. You can be conservative. Keep symbols concise and clear, and once a symbol is defined, use it consistently instead of letting it get muddled along the way. **Do not write a first draft in Chinese and then translate it into English with GPT!!!** That produces a great many unidiomatic or non-standard terms and words!!! When reading papers, remember to read them in English rather than translating them into Chinese with translation software!!! Use English for both reading and writing!!! Build good habits.

When you cannot add a conjunction between two sentences, be careful.

Take to heart the guidance from your advisor and senior labmates, and do not keep repeating the same mistakes.

Logic still comes first. Never make people guess your logic; keep the content concise and be specific. For long, difficult sentences, use "Specifically" to split up what you want to express.

Use more simple sentences and fewer complex ones; say one thing per sentence.

It is enough to explain things clearly; the point is not to show off your English.

### Grammar Issue: Third-Person Singular

- it predicts, it proposes, we propose, they ...

### Articles: A Singular Countable Noun Must Have an Article

- propose (**a**) novel method

### Simple Sentences, with Conjunctions or Periods Between Sentences

- ❌ we propose a method has high efficiency.
- ✅ we propose a method **that** has high efficiency.
- ✅ we propose a method **with** high efficiency.
- ✅ we propose a high-efficient method.

### Other Common Grammar Issues

- Given a set of queries, we first ... We then ... Moreover, ...
- in (**a**) descending order
- Note that our model supports the case (**that**) ... changed, because ...
- graph embedding **based** database partition
- rather than using ...

## LaTeX Writing Issues

- Documents mixing Chinese and English parentheses: pay attention to telling Chinese and English parentheses apart.
- Double quotation marks in LaTeX: use ` `` ` and `''` rather than `"`.
- Do not put spaces in any file or folder name in LaTeX, or in the labels of internally referenced figures and tables, and do not join words with hyphens; always join them with underscores, and add prefixes for readability:
  - `\label{fig:system_overview}` ✅
  - `\label{sec:intro}` ✅
  - `\label{system overview}` ❌
- Figures should use vector formats such as pdf and svg; otherwise png and jpg become blurry when enlarged.
- Unless there is a special reason, figures and tables in the paper are generally placed at the top! If that really does not fit, consider placing them at the bottom with `[b!]`.
- Join citations of papers `~\cite{}` and references to internal sections or figures `~\ref{}` with `~`.
- Please use `ResNet~\cite{xxxx}`; do not use `ResNet\cite{xxxx}` or `ResNet \cite{xxxx}`, to prevent erroneous line breaks.
- When citing multiple papers, use `Artificial Intelligence~\cite{xxxx, yyyy, zzzz}`.
- Note that citation rules need to be adapted to the requirements of the specific venue.
- Use ` `` ` rather than `"` for quotation marks, and likewise for single quotes.

## Issues with LLM Writing

> **Using LLMs or AI to generate content is strictly forbidden; it is absolutely not allowed!** Never do this!

### Cases Where the "Hyphen" Is Prohibited

#### Connecting Use (Semantic Dash)

Do not use a dash to join two clauses or to insert an additional note or extension.

- ❌ Example: ... closes the research loop—from literature analysis to deployment.
- ✅ Change to: ... closes the research loop, covering literature analysis, hypothesis generation, simulation, validation, and deployment.

#### Semantic Breaks or Emphasis

Avoid using a dash as a sentence pause or an emphasis marker.

- ❌ Example: This approach—though simple—is highly effective.
- ✅ Change to: Although simple, this approach is highly effective.

### Cases Where the "Hyphen" Is Allowed

- ✅ Correct: This approach is called "data-centric AI", which emphasizes the quality of data rather than the model.
- ✅ Correct: The system outputs what it calls "semantic summaries".
- ❌ Common LLM mistake: This approach is called "data-centric AI," which emphasizes the quality of data rather than the model.
- ❌ Common LLM mistake: The system outputs what it calls "semantic summaries."

Explanation: In logical punctuation systems (more common in British English or academic writing), whether punctuation goes inside the quotation marks depends on the meaning; LLMs mostly default to American style (punctuation always inside the quotation marks), which leads to semantic inconsistency.

### Avoid the Following Words That LLMs Favor

Experienced reviewers find these off-putting.

- **Describing innovation**: innovative, pioneering, revolutionary paradigm, transformative framework
- **Performance comparison**: superior, surpass, excel, remarkable, unprecedented, achieves SOTA, breakthrough performance
- **Summarizing contributions**: general-purpose, is capable of
- **Logical connectives**: notably, yet, yielding, at its essence
- **Verbs**: encompass, differentiate, reveal, underscore, surpass, show great potential, exhibit superior capability, exceed, pave the way for, highlight the potential of...
- **Collocations**: profound challenges (not a real collocation), stems from
- **Adjectives**: rigid
- **Verbs**: impede

## Writing Guide for Each Section

### Abstract: A Highly Condensed Summary of the Paper, the Core of the Article

| Sentence | Content | Key Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Sentence 1** | What important problem does this paper solve? | what (which problem is solved) + background and significance |
| **Sentence 2** | Why solve it? | why: nobody has done it / the problems of existing methods (a new problem, or an old problem done faster, better, and more generally) |
| **Sentence 3** | What are the Challenges? | Distill the challenges so they are convincing; do not dwell on minor details; the challenges should be related but not overlapping; a challenge is a challenge **of the problem**, not a challenge of the method |
| **Sentence 4** | How is it solved? | how: what method is proposed? How many important parts does it include? They should correspond to the challenges |
| **Sentence 5** | How good are the experimental results? | There must be real datasets; be specific; include a link to the code |

### Problem Formulation

1. First give a **textual description** of the problem definition so readers can understand it
2. Then write the **formulas** that formally define the problem to be solved
3. Finally illustrate the problem with an **example**, so readers understand it in depth

> A paper should ideally solve only one problem, not several. A good example is worth a good paper, and a cleverly designed running example is crucial.

---

### Framework (Method Section): The Second Most Central Part of the Paper

**Overall principle**: Use a top-down (overview-then-details) structure. This chapter is a summary of all the technology in the paper, and each later chapter follows the same overview-then-details structure.

1. **Overview of the overall architecture**: Introduce the overall Architecture of the proposed method/system; after reading this part, readers should basically understand the overall technology
2. **Workflow introduction**: Introduce the overall flow of the system/method so that readers can follow it easily; do not make it overly complex
3. **Each Component**:
   - Simple components are introduced clearly right in the Framework chapter
   - For complex components, state clearly what they do and the basic idea, and refer to later chapters for the detailed method

**Must be achieved**:
- **You must state the novelty and contribution clearly**
- **There must be an overall system architecture figure**
- **If there is no novelty, do not write it! The more you write about well-known content, the greater the side effects**
- Later chapters: first write the points with strong novelty, then those with slightly weaker novelty, and finally the weakest
- It must be self-contained, with figures and text working together, and with a leading text that summarizes the content of the subsection

**Examples of common structures**:

- A left-middle-right structure usually corresponds to "input → core process → output" and suits showing a linear main flow.
- A top-bottom structure usually corresponds to two-tier systems such as "Offline + Online" or "training + inference".

![LEAD Figure 3: example of a top-bottom structure](../../assets/lead_solution_overview.png)

**Remember**:
- If you propose multiple methods for one problem, you must be able to explain why you do not propose only the best one
- Mainly talk about your own work; put other people's work in Related Work
- Symbols must be consistent throughout, statements must be consistent throughout, and there must be no contradictions

### Experiments: The Third Most Central Part of the Paper

> The experiments section generally uses the **past tense**; other parts use the present tense.

**Experimental setup**:
- First state the purpose of the experiment: what is to be compared?
- At least 3 datasets
- Evaluation methods and metrics (latency, accuracy, cost, etc.)
- Baselines: you must choose appropriate Baselines, **and do not omit obvious Baselines**; if paper A has shown that it is better than B and C, you may compare only with A and state in the text that A is better than B and C

**Overall comparison**:
- First write the end-to-end comparison, comparing comprehensively against all baselines
- First say what is being compared (latency/accuracy/...), then analyze what the experimental figure shows
- Give how much better it is, give the reasons it is better, analyze in depth, and give findings

**Other detailed comparisons**: dataset-size scalability, compute-cluster scale-out, the effect of model size, etc.

**Ablation Study**:
- Evaluate the experimental effect of each contribution
- For example, with three technical points A, B, and C, compare four variants: All, without A, without B, and without C
- Test different parameters

**Notes**:
- There must be real datasets and convincing Baselines
- You must highlight the conclusions: what do the experiments show? What do they prove? Why?

---

### Related Work

**Choosing the position**:
- Put it in **Chapter 2**: when the paper depends on the Related Work content (it needs to use its frameworks, models, and methods)
- Put it in the **second-to-last chapter**: when the paper does not depend on Related Work (the paper can still be understood without this part). A good paper should generally put it in the second-to-last chapter

**Writing rules**:
- **Do not plagiarize other people's work. Not a single sentence may be plagiarized; you must summarize and condense it yourself (a red line!!!)**
- **Do not copy other people's wording; no sentence may be copied**
- Be factual; do not criticize others excessively, and give others credit
- Grasp the key points and do not dwell on minor details
- Be self-contained, and state clearly how this paper differs from related work and why they cannot solve this paper's problem
- Organize by category: first describe the method, then state the difference; for directly related papers, state their Limitations plainly, and for papers not directly related, criticize less and praise more
- Cite references using venue abbreviations (SIGMOD/VLDB/ICDE/TKDE, etc.) and get the bibtex from DBLP, including venue, page numbers, year, authors, and title

---

### Conclusion

- Summarize the paper's contributions, **and do not repeat the Introduction**
- Introduction: the reader does not yet know the technical details → raise the problem and the method
- Conclusion: the reader has finished the paper → summarize the contributions and significance
- Generally use the present perfect tense

## Traits of Good Papers / Traits of Bad Papers

### Traits of Good Papers

- A novel topic
- Strong technical depth
- Sufficient contribution
- A clear logical structure
- Good expression, concise and clear
- Figures and text that complement each other
- Reliable/reproducible results
- Carefully selected references

### Traits of Bad Papers

- **Delta work**: patching other people's papers, with insufficient novelty and insufficient contribution
- Others could think up a similar or better method in 3-5 minutes
- A simple stacking of existing techniques (for example stacking multiple models, many parameters, or mixing many methods)
- Related techniques that were not used for this problem but were used for similar problems give insufficient contribution (for example, if someone else used MCTS to solve query rewriting and you use MCTS to solve cardinality estimation, you will be questioned)
- **Remember: using old models and old methods to solve a problem is only recognized and remembered for the first one to do it**
- Minor tweaks to related techniques
- Methods that are too complex, hard to apply to real problems, and hard to deploy
- Confused logic that is hard to follow
- Small improvements in results
- Excessive criticism of other related methods
- Methods that are hard to reproduce, with code and data not open-sourced

## Detail Notes

- **Logic comes first**: every sentence must have a purpose, every two sentences must be connected; every claim needs evidence, and every criticism needs grounds
- It must be **self-contained**: readers should be able to understand it without any background from other papers
- Ideally there is a **running example throughout the paper**
- Each symbol has exactly one meaning; one symbol must not be used for several things
- **Be consistent** throughout, with no contradictions
- Consecutive sentences should be coherent; do not jump from one point to another unrelated one
- Why can't existing methods solve these problems? Why can the method proposed in the paper solve the challenges? You must give concrete reasons
- Word choice must be idiomatic and reasonable; one well-chosen word makes things easier to understand
- Every sentence you write must have a purpose; do not drop in a sentence that makes people guess
- **Symbol issues**: use as few as possible; with more than 6 commonly used symbols it is best to have a notation table; avoid ambiguity; concepts must be defined (where ** denotes **)
- Use `~` in `Figure~\ref{***}` to prevent undesirable line breaks

## Paper Revision LaTeX Template

When revising a submission (Revision), we recommend the following standardized workflow:

**File organization**:
```
sections/
figs/
exps/
algs/
responses/
  meta.tex
  r1.tex
  r2.tex
  r3.tex
main.tex
commands.tex
LLM.bib
```

**Additions to main.tex during Revision**:
```latex
\section*{Response to Reviewer Comments}

Dear Reviewers,

Thank you for your insightful reviews and constructive suggestions, based on which 
we have significantly improved our paper. The major revised parts are highlighted 
in \revision{blue} in the paper.

\input{response/r1}
\input{response/r2}
\input{response/r3}
```

**Common commands**:
```latex
% Mark revisions in blue
\newcommand{\revision}[1]{\textcolor{blue}{#1}}

% Right-margin note (corresponding to the reviewer comment ID)
\marginpar[]{\revision{R1.W1}}{\revision{XXXXXX}}

% Left-margin note
\marginpar[\revision{R3.W2}]{}{\revision{XXX}}
```

Each response file (r1.tex, r2.tex, r3.tex) needs a summary table in the following format: the original reviewer comment ID, the change made, and the section changed (e.g., R1.W1, clarify the motivation, section 1).
