# 4.1 Motivated Example Figure

## General Principles of Paper Figures

In top-venue papers in computer science and data intelligence (such as SIGMOD, VLDB, ICML, NeurIPS), the quality of figures and tables often directly determines a reviewer's first impression of the paper. When Reviewers evaluate a paper, "Nice Presentation" (professional layout and high-quality figures) is one of the core evaluation dimensions. **Drawing one good figure may take 1-2 days, and that is a perfectly normal investment.**

Before going into specific figure types, let us first set out a few general **iron rules of plotting**:

| Iron Rule | Explanation |
|------|------|
| **Always use vector formats** | When exporting and inserting figures, you must use PDF, EPS, or SVG. Never insert PNG or JPG screenshots into the paper; they become blurry when enlarged, which is a classic sign of "amateurism" |
| **Font sizes must be reasonable** | After the figure is inserted into a two-column LaTeX layout, the fonts in it (especially axis ticks and labels) must be close in size to the body text (generally no smaller than 8pt). Never let the text in a figure get so small that a magnifying glass is needed |
| **Keep the canvas small** | In Matplotlib, set the canvas small (for example 150x100pt), so fonts and lines naturally become larger and thicker after scaling and look clearer. Do not pair a large canvas with small fonts |
| **Use colorblind-friendly colors** | About 8% of men have color vision deficiency. Use colorblind-friendly palettes (such as ColorBrewer), and never rely on color alone to convey information |
| **Never omit the caption** | Every figure must have a complete caption so the figure can be understood independently of the body text (Self-contained) |

This section focuses on the three most important types of figures in a paper.

---

## Core Goal

The Motivated Example Figure is **the most important figure in the whole paper**. Its mission is, after the reader has read the first two paragraphs of the Introduction, to convey two core pieces of information in 30 seconds with a single figure: **"what problem this paper solves"** and **"why existing methods do not work"**.

This figure usually appears in the upper right corner of the first page or at the top of the second page, right after the paragraph in the Introduction that describes the Limitations. It is the "face" on which reviewers decide whether to keep reading your paper seriously.

## Common Design Paradigms

In top-venue papers, the Motivated Example Figure mainly follows three classic paradigms:

### Paradigm 1: Running Example + Failure Case (Most Recommended)

This is the most classic and most persuasive paradigm. Combined with the Running Example mentioned earlier, it presents a real, concrete scenario and shows where existing methods fail in it.

**Reference case**: Suppose you are studying the Text-to-SQL task. You can show a concrete natural-language query in the figure (such as "Find all professors who published at least 3 papers in 2024, along with their departments"), and then show side by side: (a) the wrong SQL generated by an existing method (missing the GROUP BY clause), together with the wrong query result; (b) the correct SQL and the expected result. Highlight the wrong parts in red and the correct parts in green.

### Paradigm 2: Existing vs. Ours Comparison Figure

Split the figure into left and right columns (or top and bottom). The left shows how existing methods work and their limitations; the right shows how your method works and its advantages. Through direct visual comparison, readers see the difference at a glance.

**Reference case**: Figure 2 of AFlow (ICLR 2025) shows the concrete structure of the three core concepts Node, Operator, and Edge, letting readers intuitively grasp the essential difference between this new workflow representation and traditional methods.

![AFlow Figure 2: example of an Existing vs. Ours comparison figure](../../assets/aflow_existing_vs_ours.png)

### Paradigm 3: Performance Preview Figure (Performance Teaser)

Place a carefully designed performance comparison figure directly in the Introduction so readers see your method's results at first glance. This paradigm suits cases where your method has a significant performance advantage.

**Reference case**: Figure 1 of Alpha-SQL (ICML 2025) is a typical Performance Teaser. It uses a bar chart to show that Alpha-SQL, as a plug-in framework, brings significant gains on open-source LLMs of different sizes, and intuitively conveys the core selling point that "even small models can be clearly strengthened".

## Design Principles

- Sketch on paper or a whiteboard first, and draw it on the computer only after discussing and confirming it with your advisor; this avoids a lot of rework.
- You can also refer to the Vibe Research chapter of this tutorial, which shares how to get AI assistance with drawing.

## Tool Recommendations

A Motivated Example Figure usually contains mixed elements such as text, icons, arrows, and code snippets, so we recommend drawing it with **PowerPoint (PPT)** or **Figma**. PPT's advantages are that it is quick to pick up and its layout is flexible; once finished, export it to PDF to get a vector figure. Figma's advantages are easy collaboration and component-based design. For figures containing code snippets, you can first generate a screenshot with a code-highlighting tool and then lay it out in PPT/Figma.

Icons for the figure can be downloaded from this site: [iconfont](https://www.iconfont.cn/).
