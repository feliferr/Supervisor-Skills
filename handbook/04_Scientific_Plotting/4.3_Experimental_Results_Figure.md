# 4.3 Experimental Results Figure

## Core Goal

The mission of the Experimental Results Figure is to present your experimental data in the most intuitive and persuasive way, supporting the paper's core conclusions. A good results figure lets reviewers quickly verify whether your method really works, while a poor one makes reviewers doubt whether your experiments are reliable.

## Common Chart Types and When to Use Them

Different experimental purposes call for different chart types. The following are the most commonly used results chart types in data intelligence and where each applies:

| Chart Type | Use Case | Design Points |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Grouped Bar Chart | Overall Performance comparison of multiple methods across multiple datasets | Highlight your method in a dark color and the baselines in light colors. Leave appropriate spacing between groups of bars, and label the exact values on top of the bars |
| Line Chart | Parameter sensitivity analysis, training curves, trends over time/scale | Use different combinations of color + line style + marker for different methods (such as solid + dot, dashed + square, dash-dot + triangle), so they remain distinguishable even in black-and-white printing |
| Heatmap | Correlation matrices, attention-weight visualization, performance matrices of modules/methods | Use a continuous color scale (such as blue-white-red) and label the exact value in each cell |
| Scatter Plot | Efficiency-Effectiveness Trade-off | Put an efficiency metric (such as inference time) on the X axis and an effectiveness metric (such as accuracy) on the Y axis. Your method should land in the upper right corner (both fast and good) |
| Box Plot | Showing the distribution and stability of results | Suited to showing the distribution of results over multiple runs. More convincing than reporting the mean alone |
| Radar Chart | Multi-dimensional comprehensive comparison | Suited to comparing multiple methods across multiple dimensions (accuracy, efficiency, robustness, etc.) in a single figure |

## Design Principles

### Self-contained

A results figure must be able to stand on its own. The caption (Caption), axis labels (Axis Labels), and legend (Legend) must be complete. A reader should understand what the figure compares and what the conclusion is without reading the body text. The first sentence of the caption should summarize the core finding of the figure, for example:

> "Figure 5: Performance comparison on BIRD benchmark. Our method consistently outperforms all baselines across different model sizes."

### Scientifically Rigorous Visual Encoding

Avoid "chartjunk" (Chartjunk). Do not use 3D bar charts, unnecessary shadows, fancy backgrounds, or complicated gridlines. Keep the design flat and clean. Color and line style must be **dual-encoded**: since some readers may have color vision deficiency, or the paper may be printed in black and white, never rely on color alone to tell different curves or bars apart.

### Highlight Your Method

If your method (Ours) performs best among several baselines, you can use a higher-contrast color (such as dark red or dark blue) for the bars or curves representing your method, and set the other baseline methods in gray or lighter colors. In Figure 1 of Alpha-SQL, "Ours" is highlighted in a striking bright color that stands out among the gray and light-colored baselines.

### Do Not Mislead the Reader

The starting point of the Y axis must be reasonable. If the accuracy of all methods is between 60% and 80%, do not start the Y axis at 0 (the differences would look negligible), but do not start it at 59% either (that would exaggerate the differences). A reasonable approach is to start at 55% or 50% and to note this in the figure.

## Case Analysis

**The problem of an oversized canvas**: A common mistake is drawing on a Canvas that is too large, which makes the text labels (Text Labels) relatively small in font size and hard to read once inserted into LaTeX. The correct approach is to use a small drawing Canvas (for example 150×100pt in width and height), so the fonts naturally become larger after scaling.

> We suggest establishing a unified plotting script template early in the project (such as `plot_utils.py`) that defines uniform parameters such as the color scheme, font sizes, and line widths. Then, when you draw multiple results figures later, you only need to swap in the data to keep the style consistent.
