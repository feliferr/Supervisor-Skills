# 4.2 Solution Overview Figure

## Core Goal

The Solution Overview Figure is the "soul" of the paper's Methodology section. Its mission is to let readers (especially reviewers who skip much of the text and go straight to the figures) quickly grasp your overall system architecture, the core modules, and how data flows between them.

This figure usually appears at the start of the Methodology section (for example after the first paragraph of Section 3) and serves as the "map" readers use to understand your whole method. A good Overview Figure lets readers keep a global view while reading the later technical details, so they always know where the module under discussion sits in the overall system.

## Common Design Paradigms

### Paradigm 1: Pipeline Diagram

This is the most common paradigm. It suits methods that form a multi-stage processing flow, shown from left to right (or top to bottom) as: input → stage 1 → stage 2 → ... → output. Each stage is drawn as a large box, and a large box can contain more detail about its sub-modules.

**Reference case**: Figure 3 of AFlow (ICLR 2025) uses a clear three-column layout: Search Space (left column, showing the definition of the search space, including the code representations of Node, Operator, and Edge) → Search via AFLOW (middle column, showing the four steps of the MCTS search process: Selection, Expansion, Evaluation, Backpropagation) → Search Result (right column, showing the optimal workflows found for different tasks). The logic of the whole figure is very smooth, and readers get it at a glance from left to right.

![AFlow Figure 3: example of a Pipeline diagram](../../assets/aflow_solution_overview.png)

### Paradigm 2: System Architecture Diagram

This suits methods that are systems with multiple interacting components. Use a large box for the system boundary, divide the inside into several sub-modules, and use arrows for the data flow and control flow between modules.

**Reference case**: Figure 3 of Alpha-SQL (ICML 2025) shows the overall architecture of the MCTS framework, including the LLM-as-Action-Model, the expansion process of the search tree, and the evaluation-feedback loop of the self-supervised reward function. The data flow between modules is clear and easy to follow.

![Alpha-SQL Figure 3: example of a system architecture diagram](../../assets/alpha_sql_solution_overview.png)

### Paradigm 3: Multi-level / Multi-perspective Diagram (Offline-Online Module Diagram)

This suits methods that involve multiple levels of abstraction (such as macro to micro) or multiple perspectives (such as the training phase and the inference phase). You can use top-bottom layering or left-right columns to show the different levels/perspectives.

**Reference case**: Figure 3 of LEAD (VLDB 2026) uses a top-bottom structure to show what the overall framework does Offline and Online, and how the Online output interacts with the Training module. A top-bottom structure is generally used when the method consists of two large modules, Offline and Online, while left-right columns are often used for linear input-process-output flows.

![LEAD Figure 3: example of a multi-level / multi-perspective diagram](../../assets/lead_solution_overview.png)

## Design Principles

- **Modular**: Represent each core component with a box, and the inside of a box can be subdivided further.
- **Clear data flow**: Mark the direction of data flow explicitly with arrows, avoiding crossings and clutter.
- **Clear hierarchy**: Distinguish components at different levels through color, size, and position.
- **Consistent with the body text**: The name of every module in the figure must match the section titles in the body text.
- **Explicit inputs and outputs**: The most basic requirement is that readers know what the method's [input] is and what its [output] is, and the figure must express this clearly.

> **Practical tip**: Whatever tool you use, before drawing the Overview Figure we strongly recommend first hand-drawing a sketch on paper and discussing it with your advisor to confirm the layout and information hierarchy before drawing it on the computer. This avoids a lot of rework. You can also refer to Chapter 5, Vibe Research, to learn how to get AI assistance with drawing.
